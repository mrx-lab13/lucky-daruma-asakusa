SINCE THE EDO PERIOD

A daruma is more than a decoration.

The origins of the daruma lie with Bodhidharma, revered as the founder of Zen. His teachings and likeness traveled from China to Japan, where they became entwined with Japanese prayer and aesthetics. By the Edo period, the figure had evolved into a distinctly Japanese good-luck charm entrusted with people’s wishes.

Its rounded form, rising again no matter how many times it falls, embodies the Japanese spirit of refusing to yield to hardship and holding fast to one’s wish until the end.

Paint in an eye as you make your wish. In that moment, the daruma changes from a simple papier-mâché figure into a one-of-a-kind talisman carrying your resolve and your wish.

起Fall seven times, rise eight

A daruma is weighted at the base, so it stands back up even when it is knocked over. Its form embodies resilience: the courage to rise again after every setback.

願A story that begins with one eye

Traditionally, you paint the left eye first while making a wish, then display the daruma. When the wish comes true, you paint the other eye with gratitude.

縁A good-luck charm since the Edo period

Introduced from China, the daruma developed its own distinct form in Edo-period Japan. From prosperity in business and safety at home to the achievement of personal goals, it has become one of Japan’s most iconic lucky charms.

福Symbols of good fortune in every face

The daruma’s eyebrows are said to represent cranes and its beard turtles, both symbols of longevity and happiness. Red is also believed to ward off misfortune, making the entire figure a symbol of good fortune.

THE ART OF SHODŌ

About Japanese Calligraphy

Experience the Art of Japanese Calligraphy

The spirit and culture of Japan in every brushstroke

Japanese calligraphy, or shodō, took root in Japan with the arrival of kanji and has been passed down for more than a thousand years. More than simply writing beautifully, it is an art form that expresses the spirit and individuality of the person holding the brush.

During the experience, choose a kanji that reflects your name, wish, or intention and learn to write it with a brush. After practicing each stroke with care, you will paint the character onto your daruma yourself, creating a one-of-a-kind piece.

BEFORE YOU VISIT

Daruma Workshop FAQ

Everything you need to know before joining our English-friendly workshop in Asakusa.

What is a daruma?

A daruma is a traditional Japanese good-luck charm that symbolizes perseverance and the determination to achieve a goal. Traditionally, one eye is painted when making a wish, and the second eye is added when the wish comes true.

What happens during the workshop?

You will choose a kanji that represents your name or wish, practice Japanese calligraphy, paint the kanji onto a handcrafted daruma, and add its first eye while making your wish.

Is the workshop available in English?

Yes. English-speaking staff will guide you through every step, so no Japanese language ability is required.

Do I need calligraphy or painting experience?

No experience is needed. The workshop is designed for beginners, and all tools and materials are provided.

Can children join the workshop?

Yes. The experience is suitable for families and children who can comfortably use a brush with guidance from an adult or instructor.

Can I take my daruma home?

Yes. You can take your completed daruma home on the same day as a one-of-a-kind souvenir and lucky charm from Japan.

How long does the experience take?

The workshop takes approximately 60 minutes.

How do I get to the workshop?
